Why won't it name the species?

Usually because it can't, and saying so is the point. For spiders, FloraFang works at the level of hazard groups: widow, recluse, or one of the common families that are not medically significant. That is because separating individual species often requires a microscope even for an arachnologist. For plants, it recognises a short list of notorious toxic species and nothing else. When it isn't confident enough to be useful, it tells you that instead of presenting the highest-scoring guess as fact.

How does FloraFang prevent overconfident or misleading guesses?

Modern machine learning models are notorious for producing high confidence numbers (like 85%) even when looking at unfamiliar noise or computer screens. FloraFang uses a four-layer defense in depth architecture to prevent this: (1) an entropy filter that detects out-of-distribution blur or screen glare and halts guesses; (2) mathematical temperature scaling that softens artificial probability spikes; (3) dual-tier corroboration requiring Apple Intelligence foundation models to verify visual markings; and (4) strict clinical framing that never labels wild organisms as “safe,” but rather “not medically significant,” reminding users that defensive bites can still occur.

What is the Field Naturalist feature?

Powered entirely on-device by Apple Intelligence Foundation Models, the Field Naturalist is an offline conversational assistant built into every saved scan. You can tap quick prompt chips (“Dangerous to pets?”, “What if bitten?”, “Safe way to move it?”) or ask custom questions. Because it runs locally on your iPhone's Neural Engine, it works deep in the backcountry with zero cell service.

Then what am I supposed to do with a refusal?

A refusal comes with what to do next rather than a dead end. It tells you how to get a better photo, or where to take the question. It is a result, not an error.

It said "not a plant we recognise as toxic." Does that mean it's safe?

No, and the app is careful never to say otherwise. FloraFang knows a short list of well-known toxic plants. Most toxic plants are not on that list, so failing to match one is not evidence of anything. Treat an unrecognised plant as unknown, not as harmless.

Can I use it to tell whether something is edible?

No. FloraFang will never tell you a plant is safe to eat, at any confidence, and that is a deliberate design decision rather than a missing feature. Ruling out a specific known toxic species is a claim a photo can support. Confirming something is edible is not, and the consequences of being wrong are severe enough that a phone camera is the wrong instrument for the question.

What does the exposure screen actually do?

It gets you to poison control quickly and helps you make that call well. The numbers appear before anything else, because seconds spent waiting on a photo to process are seconds not spent dialling. While you're on hold, it collects the things poison control will ask for, including weight and age, which part was eaten, how much, when, and any symptoms, into a single block you can read aloud. That part is useful whether or not the app identifies the plant correctly, which is why it exists.

How do I get a better identification?

Fill the square frame with the subject and get close. Cropping tightly to the organism is the single biggest thing you control. A spider on a wall can be two percent of the picture, and at that size the app is mostly looking at stucco. Use the zoom slider rather than moving the phone closer; past about ten centimetres the main lens physically cannot focus. Recent iPhones switch to the macro lens automatically when you get close enough. Tapping the subject on screen locks focus, which helps a lot on a flat wall.

Does it work without a signal?

Yes. Identification runs entirely on your iPhone and the app makes no network requests at all. The poison control numbers are built in and work offline, though placing the call itself needs a phone signal.

Why does it seem more cautious about dangerous things?

Deliberately. Wrongly flagging a harmless spider costs you a moment of concern. Wrongly calling a dangerous one harmless costs a great deal more. So a hazard warning has a lower bar to appear than an all-clear does, and the app breaks ties toward caution.

Where are my saved scans?

In the field log, along with the note you added and exactly what the app told you at the time, not what it would say today. Saving is manual, so a scan you didn't save isn't there.
